In 1961, the world witnessed a historic trial: that of Adolf Eichmann. Eichmann had been one of the key organizers of the deportation of Jews during World War II, and as such, he was responsible for the deaths of hundreds of thousands of people. A philosopher attended the trial—Hannah Arendt—and what she discovered would completely upend our understanding of good and evil. When attending Eichmann’s trial, Hannah Arendt expected to see the face of monstrosity. Instead, she discovered something else entirely: an ordinary civil servant. Not a bloodthirsty fanatic. Not a demon. Just a simple bureaucrat—someone convinced he had done nothing wrong. Eichmann did not view himself as a criminal, but rather as someone who had simply obeyed orders. He was driven not by malicious intent, but by a simple respect for authority. It was from this that Hannah Arendt’s profound concept emerged: the banality of evil.  "Evil," Arendt tells us, "is not always the product of hatred or cruelty. It can stem from something far simpler: a lack of thought.

Eichmann was a man who did not think. The state asked him to do something, and he did it—without questioning it. It made no difference what the task was, or even if he was being asked to send innocent people to their deaths. Those were the orders. And a good civil servant does not question orders; he carries them out. That is the banality of evil: blind obedience and the abandonment of all critical thought.

The banality of evil does not mean that evil is insignificant. It means that it can be committed by anyone—by you, by me, by your neighbor. Evil becomes possible when we stop thinking for ourselves—when we stop thinking altogether; when we replace moral judgment with procedures; and when we confuse a sense of duty with obedience."
